Inside the case is IBM's first laptop, the Convertible 5140, introduced on 3 April 1986.

![IBM Convertible 5140 — photo 1](../../../assets/posts/telegram-175/01.webp)

Compared with IBM's previous attempt at portability, the [IBM Portable 5155](https://habr.com/ru/companies/selectel/articles/561468/), its six kilograms seem laughable beside 13.5 kg. But the old machine really was 13.5 kg, with only a separate cable to carry. Here you add expansion modules and a rather heavy case.

The case is so indestructible that the laptop travelled safely by ordinary post from Germany to Russia, though that was about ten years ago =)

Amusingly, I haven't seen another sale with this case since, only IBM's soft bag.

![IBM Convertible 5140 — photo 2](../../../assets/posts/telegram-175/02.webp)

Like any 1980s laptop, it has a handle. Opening the lid slightly raises the middle of the case and the keyboard. The designer was [Richard Sapper](https://richardsapperdesign.com/), already known for Compasso d'Oro awards, including one for a [coffee maker](https://richardsapperdesign.com/products/9090-2/), and for his work with IBM. With the Convertible, he produced a shape ahead of its time: the austere grey-beige 5140 anticipated the IBM PS/2 aesthetic introduced a year later.

The laptop has no standard ports. Instead, expansion modules attach in a chain at the rear on a shared bus. Mine has a printer. The modules attach firmly and don't come off during transport.

To make it genuinely convertible, you needed an external-monitor module. Together they formed a docking station: remove the laptop display and use it as a full desktop machine. Watch the period advert; attentive viewers will appreciate the automotive reference.

![IBM Convertible 5140 — photo 3](../../../assets/posts/telegram-175/03.webp)

The hardware:

**CPU:** a sixteen-bit Intel 80C88 at 4.77 MHz. This CMOS chip has a static core that can stop without losing data. It provided IBM PC/XT compatibility, but by 1986 it was outdated: competitors were already using sixteen-bit 80286s. Nevertheless, the CMOS 8088 used less power and enabled power-saving modes.

**Memory:** 256 KB RAM, expandable to 640 KB. Static RAM was expensive but retained its contents while operation paused. That allowed sleep mode: pressing the power button suspended the system rather than shutting it down, preserving working data. Familiar today, but IBM were the first to think of it then.

**Hard drive:** none. Instead, two 720 KB 3.5-inch floppy drives. The format was just emerging, and this was IBM's first computer to use it.

![IBM Convertible 5140 — photo 4](../../../assets/posts/telegram-175/04.webp)

**Display:** a monochrome LCD of about ten inches, showing 80 × 25 characters or 640 × 200 and 320 × 200 graphics. The panel is very wide, making characters look vertically squashed.

**Battery life:** eight to ten hours! No hard drive, an economical processor and an unlit display.

**Software:** IBM PC DOS 3.2 on floppy disks. For the first time on an IBM portable PC, an icon-based graphical shell helped users launch installed applications without typing commands. It ran over DOS and anticipated later icon-oriented interfaces, making the machine friendlier for beginners.

![IBM Convertible 5140 — photo 5](../../../assets/posts/telegram-175/05.webp)

IBM offered basic applications: a word processor, spreadsheet, notepad, and power- and port-management utilities. The package included a Guide to Operations and demonstration disks.

In the end, modest hardware and a rather poor display. Accounts suggest IBM deliberately avoided top-end components to prevent cannibalising its other computers. Commercially, it wasn't particularly successful; Toshiba and Compaq offered 286 machines with standard ports.

Nevertheless, the IBM PC Convertible earned an important place in industry history. It showed that a complete PC could be portable and battery-powered, and demonstrated the viability of an LCD-and-keyboard form that survives today. Many also consider IBM's adoption of the 3.5-inch floppy a key step towards storage-media standardisation.
