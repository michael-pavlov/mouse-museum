### 24 | Torrington Manager Mouse | Torrington Manager Mouse
This mouse has an unusual mechanism. No ball: instead, little wheels sit at an angle to the surface.

In a sense, this is an earlier version of the Hawley mechanism; see the [patent](https://web.archive.org/web/20200703234242/http://oldmouse.com/pics/hawley/1986patent4628755_01.jpg). The mouse itself dates to 1985, [according to its FCC ID](https://fccid.io/D676J81001C), while the patent is from 1986.

Instead of optical sensors, it uses contact encoders: contact pads are printed on the disc, and little fingers slide over them. Each closure adds one unit of movement.

Notice that the outer pads are slightly offset from the inner ones. That is how the direction of rotation is detected.

### 27 | Mark II X063X | Mark II X063X
This mouse was designed entirely by Jack Hawley, a Xerox designer and the author of several mouse patents.

The positioning mechanism is an early drum-shaped contact encoder. Later models replaced the drum with a disc to save space.

There are two pairs of feelers, one short and one long. A bridge on the drum closes the first pair sooner when turning one way, and the other pair sooner when turning the other way. That is how the mouse detects direction.

To remove the ball for cleaning, you had to dismantle the entire mouse. I have no idea what the thinking was there.

The design is a brick aimed at engineers, but there were plenty of variations in case and button colours.

### 31 | HP OmniBook Mouse | HP OmniBook Mouse
This mouse appeared in 1996 as a built-in part of OmniBook and InternetAdvisor laptops. It popped out when you pressed a dedicated button with an actual mouse drawn on it.

The mouse is connected to the case by a thick plastic strip. There are no electronics, sensors or ball in the mouse itself. One layer of the strip contains three traces for transmitting button presses, but the traces aren't connected to anything: the mouse has no electrical connection to the laptop at all!

Movement and button presses are detected by a board inside the laptop, which reads the movement of the plastic strip. Another layer carries a patterned grid. Decoding works rather like capacitive sensing: the board produces a high-frequency electric field whose characteristics change with the pattern's position.

You can use the mouse freely on any surface, or even in mid-air.

### 45 | Hi-Bon Optical Laser Mouse | Hi-Bon Optical Laser Mouse
This mouse uses infrared light carried to and from the surface by light guides. Eight guides per axis provided a then-revolutionary 450 dpi.

It needs a special pad with dark dots that reflect less light.

The mouse had three operating modes:

- Standard mouse mode.
- Pressing the side and left buttons enabled joystick mode: a small movement in one direction made the cursor keep moving that way.
- Pressing the side and right buttons enabled artist mode: particularly precise positioning for drawing pixel by pixel.

Its own RISC processor handles these modes without drivers or special software.

Because of the sensor layout and pad pattern, the pad had to be aligned horizontally or vertically. Turn it 45 degrees and the mouse practically stopped working.

### 54 | Depraz, Digimouse и Logitech P4 | Depraz, Digimouse and Logitech P4
Probably one of the best-known and most iconic truly old mice.

Its greatest interest, though, is that the story of its creation traces the transition from Douglas Engelbart's wooden model to the mass-produced mouse mechanisms we came to know.

Our two protagonists:

[Jean-Daniel Nicoud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Daniel_Nicoud), a computer engineer and head of the Microcomputing Laboratory at the Institute of Technology in Lausanne.

[André Guignard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andr%C3%A9_Guignard), a Swiss engineer with a watchmaking background, worked in the same laboratory. Judging by his inventions, we would probably call him a robotics engineer today.

After Engelbart demonstrated his prototype in 1968 ([the story of how the mouse appeared](https://habr.com/ru/companies/yandex/articles/746754/)), Nicoud obtained the drawings and became enthusiastic about improving it.

A prototype with optomechanical encoders appeared in 1973, greatly improving the original potentiometer-based mechanism proposed by Engelbart. You no longer had to lift the mouse and bring it back when a potentiometer reached its limit.

The laboratory team later worked on replacing the wheels with a ball. Over several years they built a number of mechanical prototypes, but with students and postgraduate researchers doing the work, industrial manufacturing quality was out of the question. See photo 2.

In 1977, [Niklaus Wirth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niklaus_Wirth) — yes, that one — visited Nicoud and asked him to make a proper mouse for Xerox.

Guignard entered the picture and designed, for money, the internal mechanism we would see for many years, along with the domed case.

Manufacturing rights went to Dubois Depraz SA, a company established in 1901 making watches and related mechanisms, apparently on Guignard's recommendation. The details are unknown, but it seems Depraz undertook manufacturing and sales while the laboratory received a percentage.

### 110 | Daco DT420-02-01 | Daco DT420-02-01
A modern [Daco DT420-02-01 trackball](https://collectionerus.ru/collections/old-mice/219/).

Marketed for demanding vibration conditions and an extended temperature range, suggesting industrial or military use.

The idea is this: in conventional optomechanical trackballs, the ball rests directly on the shafts carrying the encoder discs. Under vibration or shock it jumps on those shafts, impairing operation. So why not separate the encoder shafts from the mechanism supporting the ball?

Here, the ball sits in a cup. The encoders attach to the assembly and pick up the ball's rotation through a soft roller.

Mine had a roller on only one encoder, and it resembled Soviet foam rubber after ten years in storage. The rollers are definitely a weak point. The encoders themselves are lovely; I'm still studying them.

There are no brains inside: it simply outputs raw encoder signals for you to do whatever you want with. The connector also has extra protection against determined attempts to plug it in the wrong way.

### 119 | Как работает световое перо | How a light pen works
In case you wanted to know how a light pen works but haven't asked =)

Our example is an ES7927.02.E000 for, unsurprisingly, the ES7927 terminal. Roughly the 1980s.

It works approximately like this:

- Inside a CRT display, an electron beam scans the screen line by line.
- When the operator touches the screen and presses the pen's button, a photoelectric sensor inside detects the brightness change at that moment.
- The pen controller records the flash timing and calculates the screen position by synchronising with the monitor's scan.

The pen was mainly used to navigate and move the cursor through displayed text. Drawing tasks hadn't yet appeared, and convenient editor line-navigation combinations such as those in Vim had yet to be invented.

Our pen has no button, but a spring-loaded tip. Pressing it against the screen pushes a little magnet, closing a reed switch and triggering a reading.

An interesting detail: the terminal-end connector is hidden behind a metal cover. It's nice that the engineers considered cable-mount wear, or made it harder to remove the pen without supervision. Seven screws are certainly harder to undo than one nut, and easy to seal.

### 126 | Ферритовая память «Минск-32» | Minsk-32 magnetic-core memory
In 1949 at Harvard, [An Wang](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/%D0%92%D0%B0%D0%BD_%D0%90%D0%BD%D1%8C) and Way-Dong Woo invented a new kind of memory based on ferrite magnetic rings. This material could remember data without a continuous power supply, retaining its magnetisation after shutdown.

By the 1950s it had become the main kind of computer RAM. It was more reliable than alternatives: resistant to interference, long-lived and requiring no energy to retain information.

Core memory looks like a set of little rings threaded with fine copper wires. The wires form a rectangular grid, or matrix. To address a particular cell, current is applied to a vertical and a horizontal wire. Only the core at their intersection changes magnetisation; the others retain their state.

A two-dimensional matrix is called a plane. Stack several planes and you get a three-dimensional memory array, or cube.

Over time, ring diameters shrank from 2 mm to 0.3 mm, and their switching speed increased thirteenfold. Semiconductors displaced core memory by the 1980s, but its exceptional reliability kept it in aviation and space equipment until the late 1990s. Some onboard computers still use it.

The photographs show a plane from a [Minsk-32](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/%D0%9C%D0%B8%D0%BD%D1%81%D0%BA-32) memory cube containing 38 such planes. Each vertical line through the cube stored one machine word. A complete block held 16,384 words, about 76 KB. Combining four to eight cubes yielded over 0.5 MB, enormous for the early 1970s.

The first matrices were assembled by hand, like beads on wire. Even after automation, the Russian term for programming ROM, literally “stitching”, remained associated with threading wires through rings.

And [core dump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Core_dump), of course, comes from there too.

### 128 | Процессор IBM 3090 | The IBM 3090 processor
1985.

One key technology in this processor was the [multi-chip module](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-chip_module), or MCM: several chips or dies integrated on one substrate.

This allows:

- Shorter interconnections, reducing signal delays between components.
- Improved thermal characteristics.
- Higher integration density: compact functional blocks increase clock speed and overall system performance.

The problem at the time was that defect rates rose exponentially as chip area grew. Making small chips and placing them on a shared substrate was therefore much cheaper than manufacturing one large die.

As production improved, larger chips became feasible: what we now call [systems on a chip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/System_on_a_chip), or SoCs.

### 131 | Mouse Systems ProAgio / Genius EasyScroll | Mouse Systems ProAgio / Genius EasyScroll
The first production mouse with a scroll wheel, introduced in 1995. It existed under two brands because Taiwan's KYE Systems, owner of Genius, bought America's Mouse Systems back in 1990. In the United States it was the MSC ProAgio; elsewhere, EasyScroll.

The idea was to speed up work in browsers and text editors, allowing users to scroll long documents without reaching for the scrollbar at the side of the screen.

Since both MSC and Genius mice then had three buttons, the third button was integrated into the wheel.

Early prototypes used a direct drive from the scroll wheel to a tiny encoder shaft. But the connection made scrolling too harsh and jittery: every touch went straight to the sensor. A rubber belt softened the jitter through the connection's inertia, though it deteriorated with age.

A year later Microsoft [released](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5912661A/en) its IntelliMouse, including the familiar stepped scrolling provided by a ridged wheel shaft.

Amusingly, Microsoft's [inspiration behind the mouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Michelman) wasn't thinking about long texts or the internet at all. He wanted to solve Excel users' zooming problems and even proposed zoom as the wheel's default function. Under pressure from [Walt Mossberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walt_Mossberg), he eventually gave in, and now we enjoy scrolling across Microsoft's applications and beyond.

The first known scroll-wheel mouse prototype appeared back in 1985. That deserves a separate post.

### 134 | Mighty Mouse | Mighty Mouse
A joint Japanese–Swiss development from 1985, ten years before the [first production scroll-wheel mouse](https://t.me/old_mice_and_hw/131).

I found fairly detailed product documentation, so we can peek into the thinking of the time. The mouse apparently never left the prototype stage; a few photographs show physical examples.

Five buttons and a separate wheel for making the most effective use of human hand dexterity. Two of the five buttons were more sophisticated, distinguishing a click from a slow, pressure-sensitive press.

The shape resembles the [Depraz](https://collectionerus.ru/collections/old-mice/173/). Understandably: a round case is the only way to fit a button for every finger.

The authors sought to standardise mice in terms of buttons and commands. Before and after 1985, manufacturers offered anything from one button, as on the [Apple Lisa](https://collectionerus.ru/collections/old-mice/207/), to three, or even dozens on exotic models. The idea was for the same buttons to mean the same actions on all computers. That eventually happened for two buttons and the wheel; everything else is extra and customisable.

The controls in detail:

**Thumb button: motion/scroll.** Browse file contents, moving forward or backward through a linearly ordered data set, such as a text file. A quick press advances one page; a slow press scrolls continuously at a speed proportional to pressure.

**Index-finger button: pick.** Select an object. A quick press selects what the cursor points to; a slow press reveals increasingly detailed information, for example through zooming. Magnification is proportional to pressure.

**Middle-finger button: menu.** Display a pop-up or context menu.

**Ring-finger button: undo.** Cancel the last user operation and return to the preceding system state.

**Little-finger button: quit.** Exit or end an operation.

**Thumb wheel: angle.** Change the cursor's angular orientation on screen.

### 175 | IBM Convertible 5140 | IBM Convertible 5140
Inside the case is IBM's first laptop, the Convertible 5140, introduced on 3 April 1986.

Compared with IBM's previous attempt at portability, the [IBM Portable 5155](https://habr.com/ru/companies/selectel/articles/561468/), its six kilograms seem laughable beside 13.5 kg. But the old machine really was 13.5 kg, with only a separate cable to carry. Here you add expansion modules and a rather heavy case.

The case is so indestructible that the laptop travelled safely by ordinary post from Germany to Russia, though that was about ten years ago =)

Amusingly, I haven't seen another sale with this case since, only IBM's soft bag.

Like any 1980s laptop, it has a handle. Opening the lid slightly raises the middle of the case and the keyboard. The designer was [Richard Sapper](https://richardsapperdesign.com/), already known for Compasso d'Oro awards, including one for a [coffee maker](https://richardsapperdesign.com/products/9090-2/), and for his work with IBM. With the Convertible, he produced a shape ahead of its time: the austere grey-beige 5140 anticipated the IBM PS/2 aesthetic introduced a year later.

The laptop has no standard ports. Instead, expansion modules attach in a chain at the rear on a shared bus. Mine has a printer. The modules attach firmly and don't come off during transport.

It provided IBM PC/XT compatibility, but by 1986 it was outdated: competitors were already using sixteen-bit 80286s. Nevertheless, the CMOS 8088 used less power and enabled power-saving modes.

**Memory:** 256 KB RAM, expandable to 640 KB. Static RAM was expensive but retained its contents while operation paused. **Hard drive:** none. Instead, two 720 KB 3.5-inch floppy drives. The format was just emerging, and this was IBM's first computer to use it.

IBM offered basic applications: a word processor, spreadsheet, notepad, and power- and port-management utilities. The package included a Guide to Operations and demonstration disks.

In the end, modest hardware and a rather poor display. Accounts suggest IBM deliberately avoided top-end components to prevent cannibalising its other computers. Commercially, it wasn't particularly successful; Toshiba and Compaq offered 286 machines with standard ports.

Nevertheless, the IBM PC Convertible earned an important place in industry history. It showed that a complete PC could be portable and battery-powered, and demonstrated the viability of an LCD-and-keyboard form that survives today. Many also consider IBM's adoption of the 3.5-inch floppy a key step towards storage-media standardisation.

### 188 | Блок СС-1М | The SS-1M module
